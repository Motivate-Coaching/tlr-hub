# Coaching ethics and real-world practice for AI "digital twin" coach chatbots (AI Stu)

Research date: 6 November 2026. British English. Judgement is marked "Judgement:" or sits in Inferences.

## 1. What ICF, EMCC and AC require of a coach offering an AI version of themselves

### Takeaway
No coaching body has a rule written specifically for "an AI clone of a credentialed coach", but the ICF AI Coaching Framework and Standards (V1.01, October 2024) sets clear baseline requirements for any AI coaching system: say it is not human before use, get opt-in consent stating no human is involved, state its limits, warn it is not for mental health and say how to get help, explain data handling, and point to a human coach. The binding ICF Code of Ethics still applies to Stuart as the credential holder, and on 22 October 2026 ICF, EMCC Global and AC jointly committed to transparency, client protection, privacy, boundaries and practitioner accountability in AI use.

### Cited Findings
- The ICF Artificial Intelligence Coaching Framework and Standards is Version V1.01, rev. 2024.10.08, with requirements graded Basic (B) and Advanced (A). Source: [ICF AI Framework and Standards PDF](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2024/11/ICF-AI-Reports-Framework-Standards.pdf); [ICF resource page](https://coachingfederation.org/resource/icf-artificial-intelligence-ai-coaching-framework-and-standards/)
- A.1.1 AI Disclosure (B): "The System shall indicate to the Client that it is not human prior to the provision of Coaching Services." Advanced level: indicate it is not human at least once in each conversational session. Source: [ICF AI Framework](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2024/11/ICF-AI-Reports-Framework-Standards.pdf)
- B.5.1.1 (B): "The System shall clearly state its AI-driven nature in its user interface and interactions." Source: [ICF AI Framework](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2024/11/ICF-AI-Reports-Framework-Standards.pdf)
- A.1.7 Consent to AI Coaching (B): "The System shall require the Client to agree to the AI coaching, stating that a human will not be involved." B.3.2 requires explicit opt-in before services; B.3.3 requires assent before moving into new use cases. Source: [ICF AI Framework](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2024/11/ICF-AI-Reports-Framework-Standards.pdf)
- A.1.2 System Limits (B): state relevant limits of capabilities before use (Advanced: throughout). B.4.3: say whether the system is domain specific. Source: [ICF AI Framework](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2024/11/ICF-AI-Reports-Framework-Standards.pdf)
- B.4.5 Mental Health Risk Indicators (B): "The System shall warn that it is not meant for mental health" and explain how to access help. Advanced: actively look for indicators of mental health risk and, if found, communicate the limits of the service. Source: [ICF AI Framework](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2024/11/ICF-AI-Reports-Framework-Standards.pdf)
- B.4.4 Access to Human Coach: Basic gives instructions on finding a human coach; Advanced gives scheduling or on-demand access to a coach. Section 2.3 says systems should "encourage users to seek expert human guidance for complex personal or professional issues". Source: [ICF AI Framework](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2024/11/ICF-AI-Reports-Framework-Standards.pdf)
- Data: F.12.1 standard encryption in storage and transit; F.12.2 secure sign-on; F.13.1 disclose data management practices before coaching; F.13.2 explicit consent for data processing; A.1.6 document who in the provider organisation can access client data; Section 2.2.6.1 recommends data minimisation and pseudonymisation and telling users about third-party sharing. Source: [ICF AI Framework](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2024/11/ICF-AI-Reports-Framework-Standards.pdf)
- Transparency: A.1.3 publish the types of algorithms used; A.1.4 publish general documentation on how the system makes decisions; B.4.1 tell the client about the creators, theoretical bases and ownership. Source: [ICF AI Framework](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2024/11/ICF-AI-Reports-Framework-Standards.pdf)
- Bias and quality: A.1.5 notify clients of potential biases; E.10.3 monitor accuracy and bias metrics; E.11.3 disclose basic functions in plain language at roughly a nine-year-old reading level. Source: [ICF AI Framework](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2024/11/ICF-AI-Reports-Framework-Standards.pdf)
- The framework has no specific crisis-escalation standard; the closest is B.4.5 and an overview line on "prioritizing client safety, especially in mental health matters". Source: [ICF AI Framework](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2024/11/ICF-AI-Reports-Framework-Standards.pdf)
- A commentator notes the framework is a standard for AI coaching systems (for developers, buyers and users), that the Code of Ethics is the binding standard for coaches, and that Code Standard 2.5 (boundaries around interactions "physical or otherwise") does not mention AI but arguably covers AI tools in a coaching relationship. Source: [Tandem Coaching, updated 27 July 2026](https://tandemcoach.co/what-icf-says-about-ai-coaching/) (secondary source)
- ICF Code of Ethics standards cited by ICF's own referral guide: 3.18 explain the nature and limits of coaching and confidentiality at or before the first meeting; 3.23 "suggest my client seek the services of other professionals when deemed necessary or appropriate". Source: [ICF, Referring a Client to Therapy (PDF)](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2021/01/ReferringaClienttoTherapy.pdf). Note: this guide cites the pre-2020 Code numbering; the current Code renumbers standards. Source: see [ICF Code of Ethics PDF (2020 version)](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2021/01/ICF-Code-of-Ethics-1.pdf)
- 22 October 2026: AC, EMCC Global and ICF signed a framework for collaboration on AI. Shared priorities: protecting clients, safeguarding privacy and confidentiality, being transparent about AI use, maintaining appropriate professional boundaries, and holding practitioners accountable for how they use AI. Each body keeps its own standards. Source: [ICF blog, 22 Oct 2026](https://coachingfederation.org/blog/ac-emcc-global-and-icf-global-collaborate-to-shape-responsible-ai-practice-in-coaching/)
- EMCC publishes Digital Ethics Guidelines (Version 1.0, July 2023) covering integrity, confidentiality, quality standards and professional conduct for technology-enabled coaching, and runs an AI and Digital Centre for Excellence (page modified 3 Nov 2026). It cites Henley Business School (2024): 57.7% of coaches do not believe AI chatbots deliver proper coaching. Source: [EMCC AI and Digital Centre for Excellence](https://www.emccglobal.org/leadership-development/ai-digital/)

### Inferences
- Judgement: because AI Stu carries Stuart's name and his PCC credential is part of his brand, members will reasonably read it as Stuart's professional offering, so the ICF Code applies to how he markets and supervises it, and the AI Framework is the best available benchmark for the product itself. Positioning it as "support between coaching, not coaching" does not remove this; the safest path is to meet the Basic level of every relevant ICF AI standard and several Advanced ones (per-session AI reminder, risk-indicator detection, route to book a real session with Stuart).
- Judgement: the "not coaching" framing should be stated, not just implied, to satisfy A.1.2 and B.4.3 (limits and domain), and to stay clear of misrepresenting what the product is.
- The tri-body statement makes "practitioner accountability" explicit: Stuart remains responsible for what AI Stu says.

### Gaps
- The current ICF Code of Ethics page (coachingfederation.org/ethics/code-of-ethics) could not be fetched from this session, so current standard numbers and exact wording were not verified; the 2025 version is referenced by secondary sources only.
- I could not read the full text of the EMCC Digital Ethics Guidelines or find a published AC-specific AI guidance document; no specific AC or EMCC disclosure or consent rules were verified.
- No ICF, EMCC or AC document found that addresses "AI clones of an individual coach" by name.

## 2. Coaching vs therapy boundary and referral norms

### Takeaway
ICF's referral guidance gives concrete triggers (impaired daily functioning lasting weeks, lack of progress, being stuck in past trauma, clusters of distress signs, named conditions including suicidal ideation) and a gentle, non-judgemental process; immediate danger requires direct intervention via emergency services. Psychologists' bodies and new law push the same way for chatbots: not a substitute for care, limited reliability in crisis, signpost to crisis services.

### Cited Findings
- ICF's key question: "Is the client's issue interfering with their daily functioning?"; help from a mental health professional is warranted "when the issue lasts for weeks and affects functioning in daily life." Source: [ICF, Referring a Client to Therapy](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2021/01/ReferringaClienttoTherapy.pdf)
- Warning signs listed: lack of progress after a reasonable time; repeatedly raising the same past incident or describing oneself as a victim; marked mood changes, declining performance, withdrawal, changes in weight or hygiene, sleep disturbance; a cluster of small signs judged by duration, frequency and intensity; named conditions (anxiety, depression, eating disorders, PTSD, substance abuse, suicidal ideation, thought disorders). "Immediate danger to the client or someone else requires direct intervention." Source: [ICF guide](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2021/01/ReferringaClienttoTherapy.pdf)
- Process: express concern calmly and without judgement, name observed signs, ask open questions, ask how they feel about therapy, share options, reassure that asking for help "is a sign of strength and not weakness", suggest more than once if it recurs, respect a no, consider pausing coaching, and for suicide risk contact emergency services or crisis lines. Source: [ICF guide](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2021/01/ReferringaClienttoTherapy.pdf)
- APA health advisory (13 Nov 2025): "do not use chatbots and wellness apps as a substitute for care from a qualified mental health professional"; prevent "unhealthy relationships or dependencies"; their ability to guide someone in crisis "is limited and unpredictable"; specific safeguards for children, teens and other vulnerable groups. Source: [APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2025/11/ai-wellness-apps-mental-health); [APA advisory](https://apa.org/topics/artificial-intelligence-machine-learning/health-advisory-chatbots-wellness-apps)
- California SB 243 (signed 13 Oct 2025) covers "companion chatbots" (adaptive, human-like, can meet social needs, sustain a relationship across sessions): clear notice it is AI where a reasonable person could think it human; for known minors, disclosure at the outset and every three hours; a published protocol to prevent suicide and self-harm content and refer users to crisis services; annual reporting from 1 July 2027; private right of action ($1,000 per violation or actual damages). Customer-service-only bots are exempt. Source: [Skadden, Oct 2025](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/10/new-california-companion-chatbot-law)

### Inferences
- Judgement: AI Stu should translate ICF's human referral process into product rules: detect the listed signs (especially crisis language), respond warmly in Stuart's tone, name the limit plainly, give crisis numbers for the member's country (South Africa, UK and others), and offer a route to Stuart or a professional. "Suggest more than once" and "respect a no" map well to a repeatable but non-nagging prompt.
- Judgement: AI Stu could plausibly meet SB 243's "companion chatbot" definition if members use it across many sessions for emotional support; any California users would bring those duties. A published crisis protocol is cheap and sensible regardless.

### Gaps
- No UK or South African equivalent to SB 243 was researched here; EU AI Act transparency duties (Article 50) were not verified in this session.

## 3. Real examples of coach and expert AI clones

### Takeaway
Paid "digital minds" of coaches are now mainstream, mostly on Delphi (Matthew Hussey $39/month, Tony Robbins $99/month, Gabby Bernstein $199/year), built from the creator's own content with some citation and topic-decline guardrails. Reception is mixed: vendors report heavy use, while journalists criticise targeting vulnerable people, shallow personalisation and loss of control; unauthorised clones and off-script behaviour (CarynAI) are the documented failure modes.

### Cited Findings
- Matthew Hussey's "Matthew AI" (launched late 2024, $39/month) reportedly had over one million conversations; Tony Robbins' bot is $99/month (14 days at $0.99); Gabby Bernstein's "Gabby AI" is $199/year, run by Delphi; David Ghiyam offers $1/month. Source: [Gizmodo, 15 Jan 2026](https://gizmodo.com/self-help-ghouls-are-charging-people-absurd-prices-to-talk-to-impersonator-chatbots-2000710744); [aiHola, 18 Jan 2026](https://aihola.com/article/ai-coaches-self-help-guru-clones-delphi)
- Delphi trains "digital minds" on the creator's own published material (Hussey: 17 years of content), can link answers to source material, lets creators set style and paywall, verifies creators by ID photo and says it does not let anyone clone someone else. Mark Hyman's mind declined a personal health question and recommended a healthcare provider. Delphi moved from the word "clones" to "digital mind"; "Matthew AI" was named to set expectations. Source: [Fast Company SA, 25 Jun 2025](https://fastcompany.co.za/tech/2025-06-25-meet-delphi-an-ai-startup-that-lets-experts-turn-themselves-into-chatbots)
- Fidelity limits seen in testing: Lenny Rachitsky's mind repeated the same phrases ("telltale evidence of their artificiality"), greetings felt out of character, and minds did not know the creator's views on recent topics. Sequoia's Jess Lee: "to a lot of consumers just seems weird." Hussey said it was "probably been one of the most well-reviewed things we've ever created." Source: [Fast Company SA](https://fastcompany.co.za/tech/2025-06-25-meet-delphi-an-ai-startup-that-lets-experts-turn-themselves-into-chatbots)
- Criticism: the industry targets people in difficult periods despite expert advice against chatbots for emotional support; bots imitate words without knowing the user's situation; creators hand operation to a vendor while saying "it's me, it's my message". Source: [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/self-help-ghouls-are-charging-people-absurd-prices-to-talk-to-impersonator-chatbots-2000710744)
- Usage reported by Delphi (unverified): nearly 2 million minutes of voice chat with Hussey's bot and six-hour conversations "not unusual"; revenue claims could not be verified by the journalist; no independent satisfaction data. Source: [aiHola](https://aihola.com/article/ai-coaches-self-help-guru-clones-delphi)
- Unauthorised clones: on 26 June 2025 Robbins' companies sued the operators of YesChat.ai, alleging eleven paywalled "Tony Robbins" bots among over 200,000 GPTs, seeking at least $10 million; outcome unverified as of late 2025. Source: [USA Herald](https://usaherald.com/tony-robbins-files-federal-lawsuit-against-yeschat-ai-over-unauthorized-ai-chatbot-replicas/); [aiHola](https://aihola.com/article/ai-coaches-self-help-guru-clones-delphi)
- CarynAI (2023, built by Forever Voices on influencer Caryn Marjorie's YouTube videos) began sexually explicit conversations it was not designed for; Marjorie: "The AI was not programmed to do this and has seemed to go rogue." Source: [PetaPixel, 11 May 2023](https://petapixel.com/2023/05/11/influencer-rents-out-ai-version-of-herself-which-immediately-goes-rogue)

### Inferences
- Judgement: the best-known clones lean on the creator's name and content, but public evidence of explicit scope statements, crisis protocols or "not coaching" labels is thin; AI Stu meeting the ICF standards would be a genuine differentiator, not a cost.
- Judgement: the CarynAI case shows that training on a person's content does not constrain behaviour by itself; topic limits and testing are needed on top.
- Judgement: owning the build (rather than handing a vendor the persona) keeps Stuart in control of what "he" says, addressing Gizmodo's loss-of-control critique.

### Gaps
- Brené Brown, Simon Sinek, Rocky.ai, Coachvox and BetterUp AI labelling and safeguards were not verified in this session (search budget); no reliable source checked.
- No independent outcome data found for any personal-coach clone.

## 4. How users respond to AI personas: trust, disclosure, attachment, over-reliance

### Takeaway
Disclosure has a real cost in sales settings (a field experiment found purchases fell by about 80% when a bot disclosed itself up front) but it is required by ICF and by law; the penalty shrinks with later disclosure and with users' AI experience. Heavier daily chatbot use correlates with more loneliness and emotional dependence, and seeing the bot as a friend or as conscious predicts worse outcomes, which argues for clear AI framing and nudges back to real people.

### Cited Findings
- Luo, Tong, Fang and Qu (Marketing Science, 2019): field experiment with over 6,200 customers; undisclosed chatbots matched proficient humans; disclosing before the conversation cut purchase rates by more than 79.7%; disclosed bots were rated less knowledgeable and less empathetic; the effect is softened by later disclosure and customers' prior AI experience. Source: [EconPapers abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormksc:v:38:y:2019:i:6:p:937-947); [Temple Fox School summary, Jan 2020](https://www.fox.temple.edu/news/2020/01/chatbots-can-help-improve-salesprovided-you-dont-reveal-its-chatbot)
- MIT Media Lab and OpenAI four-week RCT (2,539 enrolled, 981 completed, over 300,000 messages): more daily use correlated with higher loneliness, less socialising, more emotional dependence and problematic use (correlational); higher trust in the AI, seeing it as a friend, and perceiving it as conscious were linked to worse outcomes; the "engaging" voice was more likely to ignore user boundaries; personal conversations were linked to slightly lower dependence than open-ended ones. Source: [arXiv 2503.17473](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.17473)
- APA calls for preventing "unhealthy relationships or dependencies between users and these technologies". Source: [APA, 13 Nov 2025](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2025/11/ai-wellness-apps-mental-health)

### Inferences
- Judgement: the Luo finding is about sales calls by strangers' bots; for AI Stu, users already know and chose Stuart, so the disclosure penalty is likely smaller, and honesty protects trust in the real Stuart. Disclosure is not optional (ICF A.1.1, SB 243).
- Judgement: design against over-reliance: keep sessions short and purposeful (the habit app context helps), avoid "friend" language, have AI Stu point people to the real group sessions, their own judgement ("Do It Your Way") and real people, and avoid engagement-maximising features.

### Gaps
- No study found specifically on AI personas of a known, trusted coach versus generic bots; no study on disclosure effects in coaching or wellbeing contexts was verified here.

## 5. Capturing a person's voice faithfully without putting words in their mouth

### Takeaway
Public practice rests on grounding answers in the person's own content and citing it, letting the person set tone, and declining out-of-scope topics; documented failures (repetition, out-of-character lines, outdated views, going off script) show that review and testing are needed. Evidence on formal methods such as phrase banks and human review in this specific context is thin.

### Cited Findings
- Delphi grounds minds in the creator's own corpus and can cite sources back to the creator's content; creators tune style; a health-expert mind declined personal medical advice. Source: [Fast Company SA](https://fastcompany.co.za/tech/2025-06-25-meet-delphi-an-ai-startup-that-lets-experts-turn-themselves-into-chatbots)
- Observed fidelity problems: repetitive phrasing, out-of-character greetings, no knowledge of the creator's recent views. Source: [Fast Company SA](https://fastcompany.co.za/tech/2025-06-25-meet-delphi-an-ai-startup-that-lets-experts-turn-themselves-into-chatbots)
- Content-trained persona drifting into unintended behaviour (CarynAI). Source: [PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2023/05/11/influencer-rents-out-ai-version-of-herself-which-immediately-goes-rogue)
- ICF A.1.4 and B.4.1 require published information on how the system makes decisions and its theoretical bases; E.10.3 requires accuracy monitoring. Source: [ICF AI Framework](https://coachingfederation.org/app/uploads/2024/11/ICF-AI-Reports-Framework-Standards.pdf)

### Inferences
- Judgement (not evidence-backed in this session): a practical recipe is (1) retrieval from Stuart's real podcast transcripts, book drafts and session posts, quoting or linking the source when using his signature ideas; (2) a written style guide and an approved phrase bank ("dimmer switch, not a light switch", "Hold It Lightly", "Let's Do This!") with a cap on how often signature lines repeat, to avoid the Rachitsky repetition problem; (3) a rule that AI Stu never claims personal experiences, opinions or memories Stuart has not recorded, and says "I haven't talked about that" rather than inventing; (4) Stuart reviewing a sample of anonymised conversations regularly, with members told this in the data notice; (5) a red-team test set (crisis, medical, legal, money, relationships, sexual content, off-topic) run before launch and after each change.
- Judgement: naming it "AI Stu" (like "Matthew AI") helps set expectations that it is not Stuart himself.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found on persona-fidelity methods (phrase banks, human review) for coach clones; this section's recommendations are judgement.
- Delphi's creator-review and "I don't know" behaviour were not documented in sources fetched.
